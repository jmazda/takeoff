Methods

Take-Off exists to build empathy for the people in these seats: the size of the stakes, the shortness of the tenures, the weather of shocks they work in, and the real possibility of a take-off. Every number comes from public data or a stated assumption, listed here. If you are a growth economist and something looks naive, that is exactly the feedback we want.

The model in one paragraph

Each year, growth per person = a country prior + a small convergence term + the current regime (a growth spurt or a collapse) + the effects of reforms, capped + explicit shocks + noise. The prior is the country's own historical growth mean, shrunk toward the LMIC average, plus an offset for the shocks the model draws explicitly. Reforms are stocks from 0 to 1 that grow when someone works on them, decay when nobody does, and can be reversed by a successor. Their growth effects are convex in the stock and capped in total, so persistence helps and scattering does not. Policy mostly shifts odds: the chance a spurt starts, the chance it lasts, the chance an anchor firm lands. Exports and formal jobs follow income, with sector booms on top.

Siblings are four real countries with a similar income in the start year. For past start years they follow their real income data. For recent start years they are simulated with the same model and a default government, which is also what runs your country whenever you are not the minister.

At the end, the game reruns your career 40 times with your shocks and a default government, and 40 times with your choices and fresh shocks. The difference between you and the first set is your choices; the spread of the second set is your luck.

Hazards double in election years and rise by half after a bad year. Successors keep a reform with a base probability plus a bonus for each year it has run and for having a constituency, minus a penalty for controversial reforms. Siblings and the background government use the same reform mechanics as the player.

What the game does not do

  • It does not use a country's real history of coups, elections or droughts. Those roll at the country's base rates. Global shocks (1982, 1998, 2009, 2020) do follow real dates in historical start years, if you choose that setting.
  • It does not name living politicians. The player and every successor are fictional. So are the firms.
  • It does not know why any real sibling took off. When one did, the end screen says so and tells you to look it up.
  • It does not claim precision. Effects are capped so that skill helps a little, not a lot. The calibration target is that a thoughtful player beats the siblings slightly more often than a random one. Run node tools/calibrate.mjs to see the current numbers.
